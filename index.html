01

Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy

A structural collapse · X-linked, dystrophin-deficient
◆ Like you're five

Every muscle is wrapped in a stretchy bag that keeps it together. In Duchenne, the tiny bolt that's supposed to hold that bag onto the muscle is missing from the day you're born.

So every time the muscle moves, the bag tears a little and leaks. Over and over, the muscle wears out faster than it can fix itself and slowly gets weaker. It's a "the frame is falling apart" problem.

DMD destroys Muscles from within because the protein anchoring the Sarcolemma is missing. During every cycle of contract and relax, the membrane tears, flooding the Sarcoplasm with calcium. This damages the muscle fibers and the structural layers around them: the endomysium hugging each fiber, the perimysium wrapping each bundle of Fascicles, and the epimysium sheathing the whole muscle, which connects to bone via tendons.

Inside the cell, each Sarcomere loses integrity — the Z line, M line, A band, and I band distort, misaligning Actin (the Thin filament) and Myosin (the Thick filament). The regulatory proteins troponin and tropomyosin can no longer reset properly, so the crossbridge cycle and its power stroke fail, crippling the sliding filament model of contraction. The Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) mishandles signaling, ATP is wasted, and excitation-contraction coupling degrades. Damage worsens during Eccentric contraction, while Concentric contraction, Isotonic contraction, and isometric contraction all weaken.

02

Myasthenia Gravis

A broken signal · autoimmune, receptor-blocking
◆ Like you're five

Your brain tells a muscle to move by sending a tiny chemical "text message" across a little gap. The muscle has tiny mailboxes that catch the message and say "okay, move!"

In myasthenia gravis, your own body accidentally blocks or breaks those mailboxes. The message still gets sent, but the muscle barely hears it — so it feels weak and tired, and the more you use it, the worse it gets.

MG is an autoimmune attack on the neuromuscular junction. Antibodies destroy receptors for the neurotransmitter acetylcholine, which motor neurons release into the Synaptic cleft. Normally an action potential triggers that release, depolarizing the Sarcolemma and opening voltage-gated sodium channels, spreading the signal down the T-tubules to drive excitation-contraction coupling.

In MG, the signal fails, so the Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) never floods the Sarcoplasm with calcium. Without calcium, troponin and tropomyosin stay in place, the crossbridge cycle never starts, and the power stroke that pulls the Thin filament past the Thick filament cannot occur — defeating the sliding filament model of contraction. The Actin and Myosin within each Sarcomere remain idle even though ATP is available. As a result, the muscle fibers of the Muscles cannot contract and then relax on demand; the tendons receive no force. Weakness affects Concentric contraction, Eccentric contraction, Isotonic contraction, and isometric contraction alike, and patients fatigue rapidly.

03

McArdle Disease

An energy crisis · Glycogen Storage Disease V
◆ Like you're five

Muscles need fuel to move, like a phone needs battery. Your body keeps backup fuel stored up and breaks it down when you exercise.

In McArdle disease, the tool that breaks down that stored fuel is broken, so the muscle runs out of energy fast and cramps up — especially in the first few minutes. The cool part: if you rest a moment and start again slowly, a "second wind" kicks in because the body switches to a different fuel.

McArdle disease blocks glycogen breakdown, crippling Glycolysis and starving the muscle fibers of ATP. Because ATP is required to detach Myosin heads from Actin during the crossbridge cycle, energy failure stalls the power stroke and freezes the sliding filament model of contraction, causing painful contracture. For the first seconds of effort, Muscles lean on Creatine phosphate; once a "second wind" arrives, Cellular respiration of fatty acids takes over. Because anaerobic pathways are blocked, little Lactic acid forms — a key diagnostic clue.

Signaling itself is intact: an action potential crosses the neuromuscular junction via acetylcholine, travels the Sarcolemma through voltage-gated sodium channels and T-tubules, and the Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) releases calcium into the Sarcoplasm, freeing troponin and tropomyosin. But without fuel the Sarcomere cannot work, so muscle fibers struggle to contract and relax. Every effort suffers — Concentric contraction, Eccentric contraction, Isotonic contraction, isometric contraction — and the length-tension relationship is functionally useless when ATP runs out.

04

Fibromyalgia

A whole-system ache · chronic pain processing
◆ Like you're five

Imagine the volume knob for pain is turned up way too high. In fibromyalgia, the muscles usually look totally normal under a microscope — nothing is actually broken.

But the body's pain alarm is way too sensitive, so even normal touch or effort can really hurt, and you feel achy and tired all over. It's more of a "the wiring is too loud" problem than a "broken part" problem.

Fibromyalgia causes widespread Muscles tenderness and abnormal pain processing, often without structural damage. Patients ache deep in the soft tissue and connective layers — the endomysium, perimysium, and epimysium — with stiffness at tendons insertions and across bundles of Fascicles. Microscopically the muscle fibers and each Sarcomere look normal: the Z line, M line, A band, and I band are intact, and Actin (Thin filament) and Myosin (Thick filament) align correctly.

Yet abnormal calcium handling by the Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) has been proposed, leaking calcium into the Sarcoplasm and altering the length-tension relationship. Stalled crossbridge cycle turnover and a weak power stroke may leave the sliding filament model of contraction inefficient, so the Muscles feel unable to fully relax after they contract. The regulatory proteins troponin and tropomyosin reset poorly, and ATP demand rises during energy crises that may involve Creatine phosphate depletion, sluggish Glycolysis, and impaired Cellular respiration. Pain flares with Eccentric contraction, Concentric contraction, Isotonic contraction, and isometric contraction.
